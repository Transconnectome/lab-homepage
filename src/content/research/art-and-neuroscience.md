Multiple emotions can coexist in what we feel and remember, and those emotions change over time. We study how these patterns and changes in experience relate to brain activity. We examine moments of mixed positive and negative feelings during awe and emotional changes during personal recall, while also exploring new media that respond to emotion.

Virtual reality (VR) and experience reports let us study the settings and forms of complex emotions, while EEG records brain activity during those experiences. We analyze how the patterns and changes described in reports relate to neural signals. In a separate recall study, we test how emotional changes estimated from EEG during recall can guide music and video generation.

### Awe and mixed feelings

Our [study of awe](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44271-025-00299-2) measured emotional changes and EEG while participants watched VR videos. Ambivalent feelings, in which positive and negative affect coexist, were related to the intensity of awe. EEG analyses also identified corresponding patterns. The findings suggest that awe cannot be adequately described as a purely positive emotion.

Allowing positive and negative feelings to be reported together makes experiences visible that a single pleasant–unpleasant scale could miss. The study gives a concrete form to a methodological question: how do the dimensions and scales used to report feelings shape what we can observe?

### Emotion during recall and content generation

[Revisiting Your Memory](https://doi.org/10.1145/3746277.3760413) is an exploratory study that estimates emotional changes from EEG during recall and uses them to guide music and video generation. The memory content comes from participants' own writing, drawings, and music. EEG-derived emotional changes help control the generation process; the system does not reconstruct remembered scenes from brain signals alone.

This study explores a meeting point between measuring emotion and making media that respond to it. Participant-provided memory materials and changes estimated from brain signals serve distinct roles in the process.

### Related work: methods for visual and musical generation

We also develop methods for controlling visual texture and form, and musical style. The two studies below concern computational methods and do not analyze neural signals or emotional responses.

[AesFA](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v38i12.29232) separates image features into frequency components to transfer style while accounting for texture and overall structure. Our [music style-transfer study](https://doi.org/10.1109/icip61757.2026.11630156) represents sound as a time–frequency image and adapts an image diffusion model to change musical style without additional training.
