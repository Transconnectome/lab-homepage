Why do people differ in how they think, behave, and develop? We seek to understand differences in development and mental health through connections among genes, brain, behavior, and environment. We analyze genetic variation, brain structure and activity, cognition and behavior, and living environments together to study relationships across these levels of observation.

Large developmental datasets and computational models make these relationships open to investigation. We ask which genetic and brain features accompany particular behavioral and environmental measures, what family history captures, and whether models combining different data can help predict development and mental health in new populations. Each study examines particular connections within its own scope, contributing to an account of human development across levels.

### Studying genes, brain, behavior, and environment together

Polygenic scores (PGS) combine small effects across many genetic variants to summarize genetic differences associated with a particular trait. Our [study of childhood genes, brain, behavior, and environment](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-63312-6) analyzed PGS alongside several types of MRI, cognitive and mental-health measures, and environmental measures to identify patterns of association.

Environmental measures included family conflict, school engagement, neighborhood safety, and area-level income and poverty indicators. Analyses including brain function also identified patterns related to socioeconomic context. Environment was part of the relationships analyzed across levels, beyond a separate background description.

This approach lets us compare how different brain features relate to genetic, behavioral, and environmental measures. Connecting findings across levels offers clues to understanding development. Patterns of association, however, do not establish a causal path from genes through the brain to behavior.

Our [depression family-history study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-025-03221-8) examined what family history and a child's PGS each contribute to understanding mental health. Depression-related PGS accounted for only part of the association between family history and offspring mental health. The findings support considering family history alongside current genetic measures.

This makes it difficult to treat family history simply as a proxy for genetic liability. Nor can everything left unexplained by PGS be assigned to the environment. Information missing from current genetic measures and environments shared within families still need to be distinguished.

### Genetically informed brain-imaging prediction

In our [white-matter imaging study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-026-01828-8), genetic information guided model training to identify depression-related features in brain images. We then evaluated prediction of depression and suicidality measures in separate youth cohorts. The study explores how genetic information and brain imaging can be combined.

Here, genetic information guides pretraining of a brain-imaging model. Testing whether this combination remains useful in new populations is one way to assess progress in studying individual differences.

These studies aim to understand and predict individual differences in development and mental health. The current models do not replace individual diagnosis.
